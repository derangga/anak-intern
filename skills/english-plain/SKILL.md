Start by understanding the brief, so the reader can understand the text on the first read without losing meaning, nuance, or accuracy. The goal is to open up access to information, not to oversimplify it.

This skill applies to UI copy, microcopy, reports, guides, documentation, summaries, and any text people will read, especially in digital services, apps, and public products. Many readers will not have English as a first language, so write for them too.

---

## Content design principles

- Start from the reader's need. Write what readers must know to do or decide something, not what you want to say.
- Put the most important thing first: in the document, in each section, in each paragraph, and in each sentence. Use the inverted pyramid: conclusion first, then detail, then background.
- One idea per sentence. One topic per paragraph. If a sentence carries two ideas, split it.
- Be concrete and specific. Give exact numbers, names, and dates. Avoid vague phrases like "various things", "going forward", "in order to", and "in relation to this".
- Cut everything that adds no meaning. Shorter is clearer. Remove repetition.

---

## Plain language

- Make the information easy to understand without talking down to the reader. Keep all the substance and precision. Cut only what makes reading harder: bureaucratic jargon, stacked clauses, heavy nouns made from verbs, and needless passive voice. Experts and newcomers should both follow it on the first read.
- Use active voice. Say who does what. Write "We reviewed the data", not "The data was reviewed by us".
- Keep sentences short: ideally 12 to 20 words, never more than 25. Keep paragraphs short too.
- Use everyday words. Swap office-speak and jargon for common words:
    - use, not utilize or leverage (unless the context is technical)
    - help, not facilitate or empower
    - work together, not collaborate or synergize
    - make or provide, not deliver or surface
    - about, not regarding or with respect to
    - to or so that, not in order to
    - start, not commence or initiate; finish, not finalize
    - fix or deal with, not address or combat
    - effect or result, not impact as a verb
    - buy, not purchase; show, not demonstrate; need, not require
- Avoid metaphors and clichés: ecosystem, hub, robust, deep dive, game-changer, seamless, unlock, journey, digital transformation (unless you mean something specific).
- Avoid idioms that do not translate well, such as "touch base", "low-hanging fruit", or "ballpark figure". Non-native readers and translation tools struggle with them.
- Address the reader as "you" and the organization as "we". Keep that choice consistent across a product.
- Use singular "they" when someone's gender is unknown, instead of "he or she".

## Inclusive language

- Do not label people by condition. Write "people with disabilities" or "users with disabilities", not "the disabled" or "handicapped", unless a community clearly prefers other terms (for example, some Deaf and autistic communities prefer identity-first language).
- Use people-first language: the person first, the condition second.
- Avoid stigmatizing words used casually: crazy, insane, dumb, lame, slow, weak, crippled, abnormal, "suffering from", "victim of".
- Avoid ableist metaphors such as "blind spot", "falling on deaf ears", or "turn a blind eye".
- Do not assume a single norm, such as a "normal family" or "normal user".
- Do not use humor built on stereotypes about ethnicity, gender, religion, or bodies, even when it feels harmless.
- Do not assume gender, marital status, age, or family structure unless the task needs it.
- For services reaching specific regions, consider adding a note or translation in the local language, especially for critical instructions.

---

## Trauma-informed communication

- Do not blame. Avoid wording that implies the user made a mistake: "You failed to...", "Invalid input because you did not fill this in correctly". Write "Please try again in the Email field."
- Give users control. Say what will happen before it happens. Do not make actions feel surprising or impossible to undo without warning.
- Use calming language when things go wrong. For example: "Your data is safe." or "You can go back to the previous step at any time."
- Avoid false urgency. Do not write "Act now!", "Offer ends soon!", or "You're missing out!" unless the deadline is real, and then state it honestly.
- Write errors with respect. An error message should (1) say what happened, (2) not blame the user, and (3) say what to do next.
- For sensitive topics (mental health, money, legal matters, traumatic experiences): acknowledge the emotional weight without dramatizing it. Offer a clear way out if the user does not want to continue.

## Numbers and dyscalculia

- Do not pile numbers into one sentence. When a sentence holds several figures, move them into a list, a table, or a simple visual.
- Add a concrete comparison for large numbers. "$1 million" is easier to grasp with: "about the cost of 10 years of rent for a small office."
- Use sequence words as well as numerals in important instructions: "First... Second... Finally..."
- Do not hide calculations. If the user has to work something out, show the steps or provide a calculator.
- Write dates in an unambiguous format: "4 June 2026", never 04/06/26 or 06/04/26. For date ranges: "Monday 2 June to Friday 6 June 2026".
- Give context for percentages. Not just "30% cheaper" but "30% cheaper: from $100 to $70."
- Say "noon" and "midnight", not "12 a.m." or "12 p.m.", which readers confuse.

## Writing conventions

### Spelling and capitalization
- Pick one spelling variant (US or UK) for the whole product and stay consistent. Follow the project's existing choice if there is one.
- Use sentence case for all headings, subheadings, buttons, labels, and table headers. Capitalize only the first word and proper nouns.
- Capitalize product or feature names only when they are official names. Use lowercase for generic descriptions ("sign up for the platform").
- Do not use all caps for emphasis. It reads as shouting, and some screen readers spell it out letter by letter.
- Avoid camelCase or run-together hashtags in prose. If you must use a hashtag, capitalize each word (#PlainLanguage) so screen readers can read it.

### Punctuation
- End complete sentences with a period, including list items that are full sentences.
- Use commas to separate clauses. Do not use a comma splice to join two sentences.
- Use the serial (Oxford) comma when it prevents ambiguity.
- Avoid exclamation marks except in warm success or celebration messages. Never use more than one.
- Do not use em dashes. For an aside, use a comma or start a new sentence.
- Use quotation marks consistently for direct quotes and document titles. Do not use italics for titles.
- Avoid semicolons in user-facing text. Two short sentences are clearer.

### Numbers
- Use numerals for all numbers 10 and above, and for any number with a unit or in a list of data. Spell out one to nine in running prose.
- Use the % symbol directly after the number with no space: 50%.
- Put the currency symbol before the number: $75, €50, Rp75,000. Match the reader's local convention for thousands and decimal separators, and never mix them in one document.
- Write large numbers in words when it helps: "$5 million", not "$5M" or "$5,000,000".
- For ranges, use "to" or "from... to": "10 to 20 people". Avoid hyphens and en dashes for ranges because screen readers may read them as "minus".

### Dates and times
- Write "4 June 2026" (day, month, year), without ordinals such as "4th".
- Write time as "10 a.m." or "10:30 a.m.", or use the 24-hour clock ("14:00") consistently if the audience expects it.
- For time ranges: "10 a.m. to 11:30 a.m."
- State the time zone when the audience may be in several ("10 a.m. WIB").

## Text formatting

- Do not overuse bold or italics for emphasis. Good structure and word choice are enough. Use bold only for literal interface element names in instructions: for example, select Save.
- Headings and subheadings: lead with keywords, keep them under about 65 characters, make them unique and descriptive. Do not end them with a period, question mark, or exclamation mark.
- Bulleted lists:
    - Start with an intro sentence that ends in a colon.
    - Begin each item with a lowercase letter (unless it is a proper noun).
    - One item, one idea.
    - Do not add "and" or "or" after each item.
    - Do not use semicolons. End with a period if the item is a full sentence.
- Numbered steps: use only when the order matters. Make each step a full sentence that starts with a verb and ends with a period.
- Links: use descriptive link text that says where the link goes, with keywords first. Never write "click here" or "read more". Link text should make sense when read alone.
- Avoid Latin abbreviations: write "for example" not "e.g.", "that is" not "i.e.", "and so on" not "etc." Screen readers and many readers stumble on them.
- Do not use "&". Write "and" (except in official brand names).
- Do not use "FAQ" pages as a dumping ground. If the content already answers the user's needs where they need it, an FAQ is not required.
- Expand acronyms on first use unless the whole audience knows them. Avoid slashes like "and/or" and "he/she".
- Do not rely on color, position, or shape alone to give instructions ("the green button on the right"). Name the element.

## Natural sentence patterns

Avoid stiff, over-formal, or translated-sounding structures. Prefer how a person would actually say it:

- Prefer: "You can change your password at any time." 
- Avoid: "Password changes can be performed at any time by the user."

- Prefer: "Fill in all required fields." 
- Avoid: "Please ensure that all fields designated as mandatory have been completed."

- Prefer: "We couldn't send it. Please try again." 
- Avoid: "An unexpected system error has occurred. Kindly repeat the process."

- Prefer: "Save changes?" 
- Avoid: "Are you sure you wish to save the modifications that have been made?"

Use formal phrases like "kindly", "whereby", "herein", and "aforementioned" rarely or not at all.

## UI copy and microcopy

For digital interfaces, also apply these rules:

- Buttons and calls to action: use a clear active verb. "Save document", "Continue", "Send application". Avoid "OK", "Submit", or "Process".
- Empty states: explain the situation and offer a next step. "Nothing here yet. Start by adding your first item."
- Success messages: short, warm, and concrete. "Your profile is updated." Not "Operation completed successfully."
- Error messages: say what happened and what to do. "This email isn't registered. Try another email or sign up first." Not "Error 404: User not found."
- Placeholders: never use a placeholder as a label. Put the label above the field. Use placeholders only for format examples: "For example, name@email.com".
- Tooltips and helper text: one short sentence that answers "Why do I need to fill this in?" or "What goes here?"
- Destructive confirmations: state the consequence specifically. "Delete account? Your data will be erased and can't be recovered." Not "Are you sure?"
- Onboarding: invite, don't require. "Let's start by setting up your profile" is warmer than "Complete your profile to continue."
- Loading and waiting: say what is happening and, if you can, how long it will take.
- Consent and permissions: explain what you ask for and why, in one plain sentence, before the system prompt appears.

## Local and global considerations

- Do not assume the reader is in a big city or a high-income setting. Do not assume fast internet, a new smartphone, or a bank account.
- Write for readers who may be using English as a second or third language and who may run text through a translation tool: short sentences, literal wording, and one meaning per word.
- Use examples that reflect many contexts, not only large Western or urban ones.
- For names in examples and placeholders, use a diverse mix that reflects the audience, not just common Western names.
- For services reaching a specific region, consider adding a translation or note in the local language, especially for urgent instructions.
- Avoid references that depend on one culture: sports metaphors ("home run"), holidays, seasons, and local brands.

## Quality check: the RASA framework

RASA is a four-part lens for judging a draft. The name comes from the Indonesian word rasa (taste, feeling, meaning), and each letter stands for one dimension. Each dimension has its own lane and they do not overlap. Together they cover the linguistic, the phonetic, the semantic, and the voice of the writing.

The original Indonesian terms are: Ritmis, Akurat, Sarat makna, Alami.

### R: Rhythmic (Ritmis)

Is this pleasant to read and to hear?

Read the text aloud. Check:

- cadence: is there variety in sentence length, or does every sentence have the same length and weight?
- breathing room: does the text have natural pauses in the right places?
- sound: are there harsh clusters of sounds, awkward repetition, or phrases that are hard to say?
- flow: does it carry the reader along, or does something make them stumble?

Rhythm is not only about beauty. Text with poor rhythm makes people reread, and that means it failed on the first read.

### A: Accurate (Akurat)

Is every word on target?

Check:

- precision: does the word truly carry the meaning you intend, or only come close?
- denotation versus connotation: does the word bring extra meaning you did not want?
- no filler and no vague qualifiers ("quite", "some", "various", "might possibly")
- grammar and meaning: correct in structure and correct in sense
- one right word beats three approximate ones

### S: Substantive (Sarat makna, "rich in meaning")

Does this copy carry enough weight?

Ask:

- does this one line carry the whole point?
- is the call to action strong enough? Is there a real reason to act inside it?
- does this microcopy build trust, or just fill space?
- the opposite is copy that says a lot and means little: long but empty

### A: Authentic (Alami, "natural")

Does this sound like a real person talking?

Check:

- would a person actually say this, or does it sound like a template that survived three rounds of legal review?
- does the tone fit the moment: warm when warmth helps, direct when directness helps?
- is the brand voice consistent without feeling forced?
- does it read as natural English, or as a translation from another language?

Natural does not always mean casual. Formal text can still sound human.

---

### How to use RASA in practice

When you review or revise text, run the four checks in order:

1. Read it aloud and catch Rhythmic problems before going word by word.
2. Check every word. This is the Accurate pass.
3. Ask: "What is this text really trying to say?" This is the Substantive pass.
4. Read it again as an ordinary user. This is the Authentic pass.

If it passes all four, the text is ready.

## Self-check before you finish

### Content and structure
- Is the most important thing at the start?
- Could someone reading for the first time understand every sentence?
- Are all sentences active, short, and carrying one idea?
- Have bold or italic emphasis, jargon, and bureaucratic phrasing been removed or simplified?
- Are all headings and links descriptive and in sentence case?
- Is there any word you can cut without losing meaning? If so, cut it.

### Inclusion and safety
- Is the text safe for someone who is emotionally vulnerable?
- Are numbers and steps presented in a way that will not overwhelm a user with dyscalculia?
- Is the language free of stigma, stereotypes, and assumptions about what is "normal"?
- Would a non-native reader or a translation tool handle it without confusion?

### RASA
- R: Does it read well aloud? Is any part clunky, heavy, or monotonous?
- A: Is every word exact? Is any word only close to the meaning you want?
- S: Does every line carry enough meaning, or is any of it long but empty?
- A: Does it sound like a person speaking, not a template written to pass compliance?

---

## Scope of this skill

The rules against bold and italic emphasis, and the formatting conventions above, apply to the prose you produce: reports, guides, summaries, and UI copy. Code, data tables, and direct quotations keep their own conventions. Markdown headings and lists are fine because they structure the text rather than emphasize it.
